## LinkedIn

ERICK is an inclusive keyboard built for people who need something easier than tiny QWERTY keys.

Instead of a crowded key grid, ERICK uses two large radial dials. That means less reach, less precision tapping, and a more structured typing flow.

It was built with disabled users in mind, especially people with motor access needs, pain, fatigue, one-handed use needs, or anyone who finds standard phone keyboards hard to control.

Why it matters:
- larger targets
- one-handed typing mode
- controller support
- on-device predictions
- accessibility-first design

The research behind this project is strongest on motor accessibility, lower movement, and chord systems that can become fast with practice.

## Reddit

Title: I built an inclusive keyboard that replaces tiny QWERTY keys with two large radial dials

Body:
I have been building ERICK, an accessibility-first keyboard for Android and iOS.

Instead of tapping lots of tiny keys, you type by combining directions on two large dials. The goal is to reduce movement, make targets easier to hit, and give disabled users a calmer typing option.

ERICK is aimed at disabled people first, especially users with motor access needs, pain, fatigue, or one-handed use needs. It also supports controller typing, which can help people who do not want to type on glass at all.

The research base we reviewed is strongest for motor accessibility and lower movement. I am not claiming that research already proves autistic users type faster with ERICK than QWERTY. That specific claim still needs direct user studies. What I can say is that the keyboard was designed to be more structured and predictable, and related chord-keyboard studies show strong long-term speed potential with practice.

## Facebook / Instagram Caption

QWERTY works for many people, but it does not work well for everyone.

ERICK is a different kind of keyboard. It uses two large radial dials instead of lots of tiny buttons. That can make typing feel calmer, more predictable, and easier to control.

ERICK was built for disabled users first, especially people with motor access needs, pain, fatigue, or one-handed typing needs. It also supports controller typing and keeps predictions on the device.

The research behind the project is strongest on lower movement and motor accessibility. That is why ERICK focuses on large targets, guided practice, and a learnable typing path instead of trying to copy QWERTY.
