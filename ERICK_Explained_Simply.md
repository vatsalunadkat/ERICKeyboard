# ERICK Explained Simply

## What is ERICK?

ERICK is a keyboard for everyone.

Instead of typing on lots of tiny buttons, ERICK lets a person type by moving two large touch circles, or by using the two analog sticks on a game controller. Those movements are combined to choose letters, numbers, and symbols.

In simple terms: ERICK tries to make typing easier, more comfortable, and more accessible.

## What does ERICK stand for?

**ERICK** stands for **Ergonomic Radial Inclusive Controller Keyboard**.

- **Ergonomic**: designed to reduce strain and awkward movement
- **Radial**: built around circular directional input instead of rows of keys
- **Inclusive**: made to work for people with different needs and abilities
- **Controller**: can be used with physical game controllers as well as touch
- **Keyboard**: it is still a real keyboard for everyday typing

## Why was ERICK made?

Many people struggle with normal keyboards.

Small keys can be hard for people with:

- limited hand control
- motor disabilities
- tremors
- repetitive strain pain
- fatigue
- visual processing needs
- dyslexia or other reading difficulties

At the same time, even people without disabilities often make mistakes on small touch keyboards, especially when:

- walking
- commuting
- wearing gloves
- using small screens
- typing without looking

ERICK was made to solve both problems at once.

## Who is ERICK mainly for?

ERICK is mainly aimed at people who need a more accessible way to type, especially:

- people with motor disabilities or limited fine finger control
- people who find small touchscreen keys frustrating or tiring
- people who benefit from larger, clearer touch targets
- people who need controller-based typing
- people who want a more private keyboard that works fully offline

ERICK is designed accessibility-first, which means it starts by trying to help people who are often left out by standard keyboards.

## How can ERICK help everyday users too?

ERICK is not only for people with disabilities.

It can also help everyday users because:

- larger touch areas can mean fewer typing mistakes
- controller support is useful for TVs, consoles, and couch use
- eyes-free typing can be useful in class, while traveling, or while multitasking
- offline use means better privacy
- predictive text can speed up writing
- a more ergonomic system may reduce strain during longer typing sessions

This is a good example of the "curb cut effect": when something is designed to help one group, it often ends up helping many more people too.

## What makes ERICK different from a normal keyboard?

A normal phone keyboard asks the user to hit many small keys accurately.

ERICK changes that idea completely:

- it uses directional movement instead of tiny key taps
- it gives people bigger touch targets
- it can be used with touch or a game controller
- it includes accessibility options like left-handed mode, dyslexia-friendly fonts, and colorblind-safe themes
- it works fully offline, so typing stays private

ERICK is not just a visual redesign. It is a new way of typing.

## Is ERICK backed by research?

Yes.

ERICK is inspired by and built on research into:

- ergonomic keyboard design
- chorded keyboards
- accessible text entry
- motor-impaired typing systems
- Keybowl / OrbiTouch-style input ideas

The research reviewed for this project shows several important things:

- alternative keyboards can reduce physical strain
- chorded keyboards can become fast after practice
- accessible input systems can help users with motor limitations
- strong onboarding and logical layouts are important for learning

ERICK also has a dedicated research summary in this project for the academic side of the work.

## Why does ERICK have real potential?

ERICK has potential because it brings together several important strengths in one product:

### 1. It solves a real problem

Typing is something people do every day, and current phone keyboards are still hard for many users.

### 2. It serves an underserved group

Many keyboards are made for the average user first. ERICK begins with accessibility, which gives it a clearer purpose and a meaningful social value.

### 3. It can still appeal to mainstream users

Features like controller support, privacy, larger input zones, and reduced strain are useful beyond accessibility.

### 4. It is more than just an idea

ERICK is already a real cross-platform project for Android and iOS, not just a concept sketch.

### 5. It fits current trends

There is growing interest in:

- inclusive design
- privacy-first products
- alternative input methods
- assistive technology
- cross-device interaction

ERICK sits at the intersection of all of these.

## What should people understand about ERICK right away?

If someone remembers only one thing, it should be this:

**ERICK is a keyboard designed to make typing more accessible, more comfortable, and more flexible by replacing tiny keys with two simple directional controls.**

## A very short version

ERICK is an accessibility-first keyboard for phones that lets people type using two large touch circles or a game controller instead of tiny keys. It is designed especially for people who struggle with normal keyboards, but it can also help everyday users by reducing mistakes, improving comfort, and protecting privacy.

## A slightly longer version

ERICK is a new kind of keyboard for Android and iPhone. Instead of tapping lots of small keys, users type by moving two large circular controls. This makes the keyboard easier to use for people with motor disabilities, limited dexterity, or anyone who finds normal touch keyboards frustrating. It also supports game controllers, works fully offline, includes accessibility options, and is informed by research into ergonomic and accessible keyboard design.

## Why this project matters

ERICK matters because accessibility should not be treated as an extra feature added later. It should be part of the design from the beginning.

That is what ERICK is trying to do: build a keyboard that starts with inclusion, and in doing so, becomes useful for everyone.
